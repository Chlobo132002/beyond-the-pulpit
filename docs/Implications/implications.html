Public Health Implications

From Clergy Wellness to Community Health

Faith leaders frequently occupy multiple roles within their communities, serving as spiritual guides, counselors, educators, advocates, and sources of social support.

These responsibilities create opportunities for faith leaders to influence community well-being. However, they also raise important questions about the resources, boundaries, and institutional support available to clergy themselves.

The Resilience in Leadership Initiative (RELI) Clergy Wellness Pilot offers an exploratory example of how physical wellness, spiritual development, and leadership support can be incorporated into a coordinated intervention.

The following implications are informed by the pilot’s survey findings, program activities, and descriptive Fitbit data.

1. Clergy Wellness as an Occupational Health Concern

Recognizing the Demands of Faith Leadership

Clergy responsibilities may extend beyond traditional working hours and include emotional support, crisis response, administrative leadership, and community engagement.

These responsibilities make occupational well-being an important consideration for faith-based organizations.

The RELI wellness assessments identified participant interests in physical health, mental wellness, work-life balance, supportive relationships, and spiritual development.

These findings suggest that clergy wellness initiatives should consider the multiple dimensions of health associated with professional and spiritual responsibilities.

Public Health Implication: Faith-based organizations can incorporate wellness resources, opportunities for rest, and professional support into their approaches to leadership development.

2. Faith Institutions as Community Health Partners

Connecting Spiritual Leadership and Public Health Practice

Faith institutions often provide opportunities for community connection, education, outreach, and social support.

Their existing relationships with congregants and community members may create opportunities for collaboration with public health professionals.

Potential activities include:

  • Community health education and prevention programming
  • Health screenings and referral partnerships
  • Mental health awareness and supportive conversations
  • Physical activity and nutrition initiatives
  • Connections to local health and social services

However, the capacity of faith institutions to implement these activities depends on available resources, organizational priorities, partnerships, and community needs.

Public Health Implication: Supporting clergy well-being may be an important component of building sustainable partnerships between faith institutions and public health organizations.

3. Integrating Physical, Mental, and Spiritual Wellness

Moving Beyond Single-Domain Interventions

The RELI pilot combined physical wellness coaching, spiritual strategy and leadership coaching, and Fitbit-based activity monitoring.

This multidimensional approach recognized that clergy wellness cannot be understood solely through physical activity measurements.

Survey findings also highlighted interests in spiritual growth, mental wellness, supportive relationships, and professional development.

The Fitbit data offered descriptive information about recorded physical activity, but did not independently establish changes in overall health or well-being.

Public Health Implication: Future clergy wellness programs may benefit from integrating physical, emotional, social, and spiritual dimensions while evaluating each domain with appropriate measures.

4. Program Accessibility, Engagement, and Sustainability

Reducing Barriers to Participation

The RELI pilot provided wellness resources, including Fitbit devices, athletic footwear, orthopedic support, and coaching opportunities.

These resources were intended to support engagement and address practical participation needs.

However, program participation varied. Five of seven participants completed movement sessions, while four of seven completed spiritual strategy sessions.

Variation in participation and wearable-device recording highlights the importance of accessible scheduling, communication, technical assistance, and ongoing engagement strategies.

Public Health Implication: Sustainable clergy wellness programs should consider participant availability, technology access, organizational support, and the resources needed to maintain participation over time.

5. Recommendations for Future Public Health Evaluation

Strengthening Measurement and Implementation

The RELI pilot provides a foundation for developing more structured approaches to evaluating clergy wellness initiatives.

Future evaluations should consider the following priorities:

  1. Establish consistent baseline and follow-up measures. Use comparable assessments before and after participation to examine changes in wellness behaviors and self-reported outcomes.

  2. Standardize wearable-data collection. Document device adherence, recording completeness, and participant monitoring periods before comparing activity outcomes.

  3. Measure program engagement. Track attendance, coaching participation, resource utilization, and reasons for missed activities.

  4. Include participant perspectives. Collect qualitative feedback about perceived benefits, barriers, and recommendations for improvement.

  5. Assess longer-term sustainability. Explore whether wellness practices, organizational supports, and participant engagement continue after the intervention.

  6. Expand evaluation when feasible. Larger participant groups and stronger study designs could help assess whether observed patterns are reproducible and whether program participation is associated with measurable improvements.

Interpreting the Evidence Responsibly

The RELI Clergy Wellness Pilot was exploratory and involved a small participant group.

The wellness surveys included seven participants, while Fitbit-based physical activity analyses included six participants.

Differences in participation, recording completeness, and observation periods limit direct comparisons across individuals.

Furthermore, the descriptive findings do not establish that the intervention caused improvements in physical activity, health, or spiritual well-being.

The results should therefore be interpreted as preliminary evidence that can inform future program development and evaluation rather than definitive evidence of effectiveness.

Conclusion: Beyond the Pulpit

Clergy are often expected to support the health and well-being of others, yet their own wellness needs may receive less structured attention.

The RELI pilot illustrates one approach to addressing these needs through coordinated physical wellness resources, spiritual development, leadership support, and activity monitoring.

For public health practitioners, the broader opportunity lies in developing partnerships that recognize faith leaders not only as community health partners, but also as individuals whose health deserves attention and investment.

Supporting the well-being of faith leaders is an opportunity to strengthen the relationship between faith, leadership, and public health practice.
